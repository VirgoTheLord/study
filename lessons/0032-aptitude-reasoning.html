32 · Aptitude (Part B): Logical Reasoning

Module K · Part B15 questions, 20 marks, no negative marking Deduction, syllogisms, series, calendars, seating, judgement and figure puzzles.

Start here

Part B of the paper has 15 questions worth 20 marks in total (1 or 2 marks each), and unlike Part A there is no negative marking, so always answer every question, even a guess. These questions need method, not memorised facts. This lesson covers the reasoning types that appeared in 2023 and 2025; the next covers the numerical ones.

1. "Which statement must be a fact?" (logical deduction)

Take the given facts as the only truths. A statement "must be a fact" only if it is forced by the facts. If it merely could be true, it is not a fact. Test each statement by trying to build a situation where all the given facts hold but the statement fails; if you can, reject it.

Worked example (2025)

Facts: Jessica has four children; two have blue eyes and two brown; half are girls. Which must be true? I. At least one girl has blue eyes. II. Two of the children are boys. III. The boys have brown eyes.

  1. Half of four is two girls, so the other two are boys. II is forced.
  2. I: could both girls have brown eyes (and both boys blue)? Yes, nothing forbids it. So I need not be true.
  3. III: the boys could be the blue-eyed ones. Not forced.

Answer: II only.

Worked example (2023)

Facts: pictures can tell a story; all storybooks have pictures; some storybooks have words. III: "Some storybooks have both words and pictures." Take the storybooks that have words; every storybook has pictures, so those have both. III is forced. I ("pictures tell stories better than words") and II ("the stories are very simple") are opinions, not forced. Answer: III only.

Worked example (2025): "must be true"

Offices are in buildings of 2 to 8 floors. If a building has more than 3 floors, it has a lift. A 7th floor can only exist in a building with at least 7 floors, which is more than 3, so that building has a lift. So 7th floors have lifts. "2nd floors do not have lifts" is not forced (a tall building has a 2nd floor too), and the last option over-claims.

2. Syllogisms: "statements and conclusions"

Draw circles. All A are B: A's circle inside B's. Some A are B: the circles overlap. No A is B: separate circles. Remember: "some" and "no" can be read both ways ("Some A are B" ⇒ "Some B are A"), but "all" cannot ("All A are B" does not give "All B are A"). A conclusion follows only if it is true in every drawing that fits the statements.

Worked example (2025)

Statements: Some books are tables. Some tables are mirrors. Conclusions: I. Some mirrors are books. II. No book is a mirror.

The books circle and the mirrors circle may or may not overlap, since the only link is through "tables". So I is not definitely true, and II is not definitely true. But the two conclusions are complementary: "some mirrors are books" and "no book is a mirror" cannot both be false (either there is an overlap or there isn't). When neither follows alone but together they cover every possibility, the answer is "either I or II follows".

Worked example (2025)

Statements: No woman teacher can play. Some women teachers are athletes. Conclusions: I. Male athletes can play. II. Some athletes can play.

From the statements we learn that some athletes (those who are women teachers) cannot play. We learn nothing about male athletes, and nothing says any athlete can play. Neither is forced: neither I nor II follows.

3. Statements and courses of action; critical reasoning

  • Course of action: a valid course is a sensible, practical step that directly helps solve the problem. Extreme, punitive or irrelevant steps ("suspend the officers", "tell the regulator to stop giving directives") do not follow. In the 2023 example, neither follows; a reasonable action would be to investigate the complaint.
  • Completing an argument: find the logical gap. "Two-fifths admitted to being at least a little dishonest. The survey may underestimate dishonest applicants because…": the survey counts what people say. It underestimates if some dishonest people claimed to be honest, which is the first option. The others are about people overstating dishonesty, or about how much dishonest, not how many.
  • Situational judgement: choose the answer that is honest, communicates early to the right person, and offers a solution (speak to your manager, explain, ask or propose cover). Avoid deceit (calling in sick), dodging (pretending you didn't see) or passing your duty onto colleagues without asking the manager.

4. Number series and "find the wrong number"

Look at the differences, ratios and combined patterns, in this order: (1) differences, (2) differences of differences, (3) ratios or "×n + n", (4) squares/cubes, (5) alternating patterns.

Worked example (2023): the wrong number in 7, 8, 18, 57, 228, 1165, 6996

The numbers grow fast, so try "multiply and add". 7 × 1 + 1 = 8. 8 × 2 + 2 = 18. 18 × 3 + 3 = 57. 57 × 4 + 4 = 232, but the series says 228. Continue from 232: 232 × 5 + 5 = 1165 ✓ and 1165 × 6 + 6 = 6996 ✓. So the wrong number is 228 (it should be 232).

5. Calendar: what day was it?

A week repeats every 7 days, so only the remainder when dividing the number of days by 7 (the "odd days") matters. An ordinary year has 365 days = 52 weeks + 1 odd day; a leap year has 2. Start from a date whose weekday you know. A useful anchor: 1 January 2000 was a Saturday.

Worked example (2025): 28 May 2006
  1. From 1 Jan 2000 to 1 Jan 2006: years 2000 (leap, 2), 2001 (1), 2002 (1), 2003 (1), 2004 (leap, 2), 2005 (1) = 8 odd days = 1 odd day. Saturday + 1 = Sunday = 1 Jan 2006.
  2. From 1 Jan to 28 May 2006: Jan 31 + Feb 28 + Mar 31 + Apr 30 = 120 days, plus 27 more days to reach 28 May = 147 days.
  3. 147 = 21 weeks exactly, so the weekday is the same: Sunday.

6. Seating and arrangement puzzles

  1. Draw the shape (row, circle, square table) and mark positions.
  2. Settle the directions: facing the centre means your right hand points counter-clockwise around the table when you look from above; facing outside, the opposite. Check how the question defines "right".
  3. Place the definite clues first (exact positions, "third to the right of").
  4. Then use negative clues ("not beside") to eliminate seats.
  5. Only when one arrangement is left, answer the question.

The 2023 paper had an eight-person square-table puzzle (4 at corners facing the centre, 4 at mid-sides facing out, with 9 clues). It is a longer version of the same procedure, so practise it on paper.

7. Venn diagrams and set counting

For two sets: |A ∪ B| = |A| + |B| − |A ∩ B|. "Football or cricket but not basketball" means the regions that are in football or cricket and outside basketball. Fill the regions of the Venn diagram from the centre outward (the triple overlap first), then subtract.

8. Figure and pattern questions (non-verbal)

Several questions each year ask "which figure replaces the question mark", "which completes the series", "mirror image of this". They need no formula, only a checklist:

  • Does something rotate (by a fixed angle each step)? Move position? Flip?
  • Does a count increase or decrease by a fixed amount (sides, dots, lines)?
  • Does shading alternate or move?
  • For grids/boxes: look across a row (or down a column): is the third a sum, difference or overlay of the first two?
  • Mirror image: a mirror on the right swaps left and right (letters are reversed left-to-right but stay upright); a mirror below swaps top and bottom.

With no negative marking in Part B, never leave a figure question blank: eliminate the options that break a rule you've found, then guess among the rest.
